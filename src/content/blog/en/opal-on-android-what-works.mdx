**Yes. Opal has been on Google Play since mid-2025**, as Opal Screen Time Limit & Timer by Opal: The
Focus Company, with 500K+ downloads. It needs **Android 10 or newer**.

The useful question is not whether it exists but what is different about it, and nearly all of that
traces back to one thing: Android has no equivalent of Apple's Screen Time API.

## The facts, from the Play listing

| | Opal on Android |
|---|---|
| Available since | Mid-2025 |
| Downloads | 500K+ |
| Minimum Android version | Android 10 |
| How it blocks | AccessibilityService API (on Android 14+) |
| Data | "Your data never leaves your device," per the listing |
| Price | Free download, limited free tier; Pro by subscription |
| In-app purchases | $2.99 to $79.99 per item |

Checked against the [Google Play listing](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.withopal.opal)
in October 2026. The Data safety line is the developer's own declaration, as it is for every app, not
an independent audit.

## The one real difference from the iPhone version

On iOS, Apple gives focus apps the **Screen Time API**. Blocking happens at the system level, and the
app you blocked simply does not open.

Android has nothing equivalent. Every blocker on the platform — Opal, AppBlock,
[Mindful Guard](/apps/mindful-guard), all of them — falls back to the **AccessibilityService API**,
which watches for a blocked app coming to the foreground and reacts. It works well. It is also
slightly less absolute, because it is reacting rather than preventing.

This is a platform constraint, not an Opal flaw. Judging an Android blocker by whether it feels as
hard as iOS is judging it by something no Android app can deliver.

## What that means day to day

- **Blocking is a beat slower.** The app opens for a fraction of a second before the block screen
  takes over. Every accessibility-based blocker does this.
- **The permission is broad.** The accessibility service can see what is on screen. That is the price
  of the only mechanism Android offers, and it is worth checking what an app does with it — see
  [why a blocker needs that permission](/blog/how-to-block-websites-on-android-chrome).
- **Battery is not the issue.** Accessibility-based blocking is cheap; the worry comes from Opal's
  pre-2022 VPN era. Full version in [does Opal drain battery](/blog/does-opal-drain-battery).
- **Updates can break and fix things.** Reviews reported a mid-2026 issue where newly installed apps
  were not blocked until the phone restarted, apparently fixed in the 15 July 2026 release. Worth
  knowing as a category risk rather than an Opal-specific one.

## Is the Android version worth it?

Depends entirely on the free tier, because that is what you get before you decide. One blocking rule,
basic timers, your daily score — and the locked session that cannot be ended early is Pro. The
pricing picture is genuinely murky, which is why it has
[its own page](/blog/is-opal-free).

If Opal's polish and streaks appeal and one rule covers your problem, it is a reasonable choice. If
you want scheduling, feed-level limits or a no-override block without paying, the comparison against
AppBlock, Freedom and Mindful Guard is in
[the best Opal alternatives for Android](/blog/opal-alternatives-android).

## Which Opal are you looking for?

Several unrelated products share the name, which is why searches in this space return strange
results: a **webcam**, the Sydney **public transport card**, and various others.

The focus app is **Opal Screen Time Limit & Timer**, by **Opal: The Focus Company**. If you arrived
here looking for one of the others, this is not it.

## The bottom line

Opal is on Android, it is real, and it works. The one structural difference — accessibility service
instead of a Screen Time API — applies to every Android blocker equally, so it is not a reason to
pick one over another.

What should decide it is the free tier and the price, and on the price nobody publishing a confident
number agrees with anyone else.
