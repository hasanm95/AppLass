**The best app blocker for studying is one you cannot talk your way out of halfway through a session.**
Every phone has some way to pause apps. What separates a study tool from a suggestion is whether it holds
when you are bored, stuck on a problem, and one tap away from Instagram.

**Quick answer:** on Android, use a focus timer with **no early exit** for each session, a **weekly
schedule** for your regular study hours, and put the phone **out of sight**. Mindful Guard does the first
two on its free plan.

## Your phone hurts concentration even when you are not using it

**Having your phone within reach lowers your available attention, even if it is face down and silent.** In
[Ward and colleagues' 2017 "Brain Drain" study](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdfplus/10.1086/691462),
about 800 people took tests of attention and working memory with their phones either on the desk, in a
pocket or bag, or in another room.

| Where the phone was | Test performance |
|---|---|
| On the desk | Lowest |
| In a pocket or bag | Slightly better |
| In another room | Best, significantly better than on the desk |

Whether the phone was on or off, face up or face down, made no difference. The researchers' explanation is
that part of your mind is busy *not* picking it up. For studying, that has a practical meaning: blocking
apps helps, but **blocking apps and moving the phone** helps more.

## What makes an app blocker good for studying?

Look for four things, in this order:

1. **No early exit.** Once a session starts, it should not be possible to end it from inside the app. This
   is the feature that matters, because the urge to quit peaks in the middle of a hard problem.
2. **Quick sessions.** You should be able to start a 25- or 50-minute block in two taps, without building a
   schedule.
3. **Recurring schedules.** Study hours that repeat every weekday should switch on by themselves.
4. **Blocking the right thing.** Sometimes you need messaging for a group project. A blocker that can block
   the Reels or Shorts feed but leave messages working is more useful than an all-or-nothing lock.

## A study setup that actually holds

Here is a setup you can copy. It takes about five minutes.

1. **Pick your block list.** Social media, YouTube, games — whatever you open by reflex. If you need
   messaging, block only the feeds.
2. **Create a weekly schedule** for your regular study hours, for example weekdays 4 to 7 p.m. and weekend
   mornings.
3. **Turn on strict mode** so sessions cannot be ended early.
4. **For extra sessions, use the quick timer.** 25 minutes on, 5 off, four rounds, then a longer break — the
   classic Pomodoro rhythm — or 50/10 for deep work.
5. **Put the phone in a bag or another room** during the session. The study above says this matters even
   with apps blocked.
6. **Schedule your phone breaks.** Knowing you will check messages at 5:30 removes most of the itch to check
   them at 5:10.

## Exam season: going stricter

In the last weeks before exams, many students go further:

- **Extend schedules to cover the whole day** except planned breaks.
- **Block the app store and browser** if you tend to reinstall apps or use the web version.
- **Use a basic alarm, not your phone, to wake up**, so the first thing you see in the morning is not a feed.
  If you sleep through alarms, a mission alarm such as
  [Arisely](/blog/arisely-alarm-clock-for-heavy-sleepers) gets you up without the phone in bed.
- **Protect sleep.** Memory consolidation happens during sleep; an all-nighter with a blocker is still an
  all-nighter. Our guide on [stopping late-night scrolling](/blog/how-to-stop-doomscrolling-at-night) helps.
