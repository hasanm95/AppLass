**To stop doomscrolling at night, stop expecting yourself to decide at midnight.** By then you are tired,
your self-control is at its lowest, and the feed has no bottom. The setup that works makes the decision
earlier in the day, when you are thinking clearly, and lets the phone enforce it.

Below: why night scrolling is so sticky, what it does to sleep, and a five-step Android setup that ends it.

## Why is it so hard to stop scrolling at night?

**Because the feed never ends and you are at your weakest.** Three things stack up:

- **No stopping cue.** A TV episode ends. A chapter ends. Reels, Shorts and TikTok are built to keep going,
  so the only way out is a decision, every time.
- **Tired brains choose the easy option.** Late in the day, people find it harder to resist an immediate
  reward for a delayed one. "One more video" beats "sleep that pays off tomorrow."
- **Bedtime procrastination.** Many people treat the hour before bed as the only time that is theirs and
  delay sleep to keep it — sometimes called *revenge bedtime procrastination*. In a
  [survey of 160 Malaysian undergraduates](https://hrmars.com/ijarbss/article/view/26641/The-Cost-of-Midnight-Scrolling-Smartphone-Induced-Bedtime-Procrastination-as-a-Predictor-of-Daytime-Sleepiness-and-Life-Satisfaction),
  43.75% reported a high level of bedtime procrastination caused by phone use, and it predicted more
  daytime sleepiness.

None of this is a character flaw. It is a predictable result of putting an endless feed in front of a
tired person.

## What does late-night scrolling do to sleep?

**Mostly, it makes you go to bed later.** The research is clearer on that than on anything else:

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Heavy phone users in the hours before bed slept **about 40 minutes later** than light users; 65% said screens kept them from going to bed on time | [NUS Medicine, Singapore, 2021](https://medicine.nus.edu.sg/?p=25928) |
| 90.6% of 245 adolescents used phones in bed, for **2.3 hours a day** on average; bedtime procrastination linked phone use to worse sleep quality | [Adolescent study, 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11059092/) |
| Reading on a light-emitting tablet for four hours before bed delayed melatonin and made it **take longer to fall asleep** than reading a printed book | [Chang et al., *PNAS*, 2015](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1418490112) |

Two honest caveats. Much of this research is correlational — people who sleep badly may also scroll more.
And while screen light gets most of the attention, the bigger effect in everyday life is probably simpler:
**every minute you scroll is a minute you are not asleep**. A dim phone at arm's length matters less than
the 45 minutes it ate.

## The five-step setup that ends night scrolling

Each step works without willpower. Do as many as you can; the first two do most of the work.

### 1. Schedule a block on the feeds for your bedtime window

Pick a start time 30 to 60 minutes before you want to be asleep, and block the apps that keep you up for
the whole night, every night. You do not have to block everything. If the problem is Reels and Shorts, block
just those and keep messages working.

On Android, a **scheduled** block matters more than a timer. App timers in Digital Wellbeing can be dismissed
with one tap — exactly the tap you will make at 12:40 a.m. A scheduled block in an app blocker holds until
morning. [Mindful Guard](/apps/mindful-guard) does this with a Sleep schedule (for example 11 p.m. to 7 a.m.),
and its Reel Guard can block only the short-video feed inside Instagram, YouTube and TikTok.

### 2. Turn on Bedtime mode

Android's **Bedtime mode** (Settings → Digital Wellbeing & parental controls → Bedtime mode) can turn the
screen grayscale and switch on Do Not Disturb on a schedule. Grayscale sounds trivial, but a gray feed is
noticeably less compelling than a colorful one. It does not block anything, so pair it with step 1.

### 3. Charge the phone out of reach

Put the charger across the room, or in another room. If the phone is not in your hand when you lie down,
you have to get up to start scrolling — which is a decision, and decisions are where you win.

### 4. Get a real alarm so the phone has no reason to be in bed

The most common reason people keep the phone by the pillow is the alarm. Move the alarm across the room with
the phone, or use an alarm that needs you to get up anyway. An alarm like
[Arisely](/blog/arisely-alarm-clock-for-heavy-sleepers), which keeps ringing until you finish a mission such
as walking a set number of steps, works well with a phone on the far side of the room.

### 5. Give the evening a different ending

Revenge bedtime procrastination is about wanting time for yourself. Give yourself that time earlier — a fixed
hour after dinner that is genuinely yours — and replace the last 20 minutes in bed with something that ends:
a chapter of a book, a podcast episode on a sleep timer, a short stretch.

## What to do when you catch yourself scrolling anyway

The setup reduces the nights, it does not make them impossible. When you notice you are 20 minutes deep:

- **Put the phone face down, across the room**, not on the nightstand.
- **Do not check the time.** Calculating how little sleep you will get makes it harder to fall asleep.
- **Move the block 15 minutes earlier tomorrow** instead of promising to try harder.
