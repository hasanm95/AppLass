**Dark mode saves 3–9% of your phone's total power at the brightness most people actually use.**
Not 60%, not half your battery. The large numbers you have seen quoted are real, but they only
apply at maximum brightness — a setting most people reach for outdoors in direct sun, and rarely
otherwise.

That gap between 3% and 47% is not a contradiction. It is the whole story of how OLED power works,
and understanding it tells you what genuinely extends your screen-on time.

---

## Does dark mode save battery on AMOLED?

**Yes, but modestly under normal use.** Researchers at Purdue University measured 3–9% savings in
total phone power when switching from light to dark mode at 30–50% brightness. At 100% brightness,
the same switch saved 39–47%. The effect is real; its size depends almost entirely on how bright
your screen is.

The source is *How much battery does dark mode save? An accurate OLED display power profiler for
modern smartphones* by Pranab Dash and Professor Y. Charlie Hu of Purdue's Elmore Family School of
Electrical and Computer Engineering, published at
[MobiSys '21](https://doi.org/10.1145/3458864.3467682) and summarised in
[Purdue's announcement](https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/archive/releases/2021/Q3/dark-mode-may-not-save-your-phones-battery-life-as-much-as-you-think,-but-there-are-a-few-silver-linings.html).
They tested four generations of Android hardware — Pixel 2, Moto Z3, Pixel 4, and Pixel 5 — across
sixty-second sessions in six Google apps including Maps, News, and YouTube.

The methodology is why these numbers are worth trusting. Android's own battery utility is blind to
screen content and reports identical display energy in light and dark mode, so it cannot measure
this effect at all. The Purdue tool isolates the OLED display's actual share of drain, which is what
the manufacturer demos that preceded it never did.

| Screen brightness | Battery saved by dark mode | Typical situation |
|---|---|---|
| 100% | 39–47%, averaging 42% | Outdoors, direct sunlight |
| 50% | 8.5% | Indoors, bright room |
| 30% | Under 5% | Indoors, dim or evening use |

## Why brightness decides everything

On an OLED or AMOLED panel there is no backlight. Every pixel emits its own light and draws its own
current, and that draw rises with brightness. A black pixel is simply switched off, costing
essentially nothing.

That means the display's share of total power is not fixed. The Purdue team measured the OLED
display consuming **44–73% of the phone's entire power draw** in light mode at maximum brightness.
Turn brightness down and the display shrinks to a much smaller slice of the pie — so darkening
pixels removes a much smaller absolute amount, even though the percentage of pixels you darkened is
identical.

This is also why auto-brightness quietly undermines dark mode. Most people never sit at 100%; the
sensor keeps them somewhere in the range where the savings are single digits.

## Dark mode is not a black screen

Here is the distinction almost every article on this topic misses. **Dark mode does not turn pixels
off — it changes which pixels are lit.** A dark-themed app still illuminates text, icons,
thumbnails, images, buttons, and interface chrome. Depending on the app, that can be most of the
screen. Dark mode swaps a bright background for a dark one and leaves everything else glowing.

A fully black screen is a different mechanism. Every pixel is off, so the display cost drops to
near zero rather than being reduced by a fraction. That is why the ceiling for a blackout is the
display's entire share of power draw — that 44–73% figure — while dark mode's ceiling is the
39–47% the study measured.

The practical version of this: if you are listening to a two-hour podcast or a lecture on YouTube,
dark mode saves you single digits, because the display is still on and still lit. Blacking the
screen out entirely while the audio continues removes the display from the equation. That is the
mechanism behind the
[YouTube screen-off battery approach](/blog/youtube-screen-off-battery-hack), and it is a different
thing from choosing a dark theme.

## Dark mode on an LCD saves nothing

If your phone has an LCD panel, dark mode has no meaningful battery benefit at all. LCDs light
every pixel using one backlight that runs at the same intensity regardless of what is on screen. A
black pixel is produced by blocking that light, not by switching it off — so it costs exactly as
much as a white one.

Dark mode on an LCD is worth using for eye comfort at night, and for nothing else. Check your
phone's display specification before you expect savings: OLED, AMOLED, POLED, and Super AMOLED all
qualify; IPS and LCD do not.

## What actually reduces display drain, ranked

| Action | Effect on battery | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| Lower screen brightness | Largest single lever, works on every panel type | One swipe |
| Turn the display off during audio-only use | Removes nearly all display draw on OLED | One tap with an overlay app |
| Reduce screen timeout | Cuts accidental screen-on time | One setting |
| Dark mode | 3–9% typical, up to 39–47% at full brightness on OLED | One setting |
| Lower refresh rate (120Hz → 60Hz) | Modest, panel-dependent | One setting |
| Black wallpaper | Only while the home screen is visible | One change |

The ordering surprises people, but it follows directly from the physics: brightness is the
multiplier on every lit pixel, so changing it beats changing which pixels are lit.

## Test it on your own phone

The numbers above are averages across four specific phones. Yours will differ, and it is easy to
measure:

1. Charge to 100% and **turn off auto-brightness** — this matters more than every other step
   combined.
2. Set a fixed brightness you actually use, for example 40%.
3. Play the same content for one hour in light mode. Note the battery percentage drop.
4. The next day, repeat with identical brightness and content in dark mode.
5. Compare. If the difference is a couple of percent, that is the correct result at that
   brightness — not a failed test.

Anyone reporting dramatic savings without stating their brightness level has not really measured
anything, which is the core reason the "dark mode saves half your battery" claim spread so widely.

## Bottom line

Dark mode is worth using. It is easier on the eyes at night, and on an OLED phone it saves a real
if small amount of power — 3–9% in normal indoor conditions, considerably more if you spend time
outdoors at full brightness.

What it is not is a battery strategy. If your goal is screen-on time, brightness is the lever. And
if you are using your phone for audio while the screen sits there lit and ignored, the honest
answer is not a darker theme — it is no picture at all.
