## Freedom (Best for multi-device blocking)

Freedom is the old guard of the focus industry and still one of the most powerful tools available. Its biggest selling point is that it can lock down your phone, your Mac, and your Windows PC at the exact same time.

For writers, programmers, or remote workers who switch devices just to check Twitter or Reddit, Freedom builds a unified wall across your entire digital life. The mobile interface feels a little dated, though, and the setup isn't as smooth as newer apps — users occasionally report confusing schedule conflicts that lock them out unintentionally.

- **Who it's for:** Professionals who need cross-platform syncing across laptop, tablet, and phone simultaneously.
- **Who should skip it:** Mobile-only users who want a sleek interface, or anyone looking for a completely free solution.
- **Pricing:** Typically around $39.99/year (check their site for current rates).

## AppBlock (Best for heavy customization)

AppBlock is one of the most downloaded productivity apps built specifically for Android. It takes a highly customizable approach, letting you create complex rules based on time, Wi-Fi network, or physical location — for example, blocking TikTok automatically when you arrive at the office.

Its Strict Mode is robust and genuinely hard to cheat. But the free version limits how many schedules you can create and frequently prompts you to upgrade. On devices with aggressive battery savers like Xiaomi or Samsung, AppBlock can also be killed in the background, unexpectedly unblocking your apps.

- **Who it's for:** Power users who want blocks triggered by location or specific Wi-Fi networks.
- **Who should skip it:** People who want a generous free tier, since the free version is heavily restricted.
- **Pricing:** Freemium, with premium around $29.99/year.

## One Sec (Best for mindful friction)

One Sec takes a radically different psychological approach. Instead of locking you out, it forces you to pause, take a deep breath, and wait a few seconds before an app opens.

That friction breaks the subconscious "dopamine loop" — you quickly notice how often you open Instagram purely by muscle memory. It's brilliant for mild habits. But for deep-rooted phone addiction, a 10-second breathing exercise isn't enough; many users just wait out the animation and doomscroll anyway.

- **Who it's for:** Users with mild screen-time issues who want to build better habits through mindfulness.
- **Who should skip it:** People with severe phone addiction or ADHD who need a hard, unbreakable lock.
- **Pricing:** Free basic version; premium tier for advanced syncing.

## Digital Wellbeing (Best for built-in basics)

If you own an Android, you already have an Opal alternative installed. Google's Digital Wellbeing is built directly into system settings.

It lets you set daily app timers (say, 30 minutes of YouTube) and uses Wind Down/Bedtime mode to grey your screen at night. It's completely free, needs no setup, and sits quietly in the background. The catch: there's no strict mode. Hit your limit and you can tap "add 5 more minutes" or disable the timer entirely in seconds.

- **Who it's for:** Beginners who want basic screen-time tracking without installing anything new.
- **Who should skip it:** Anyone who knows they'll cheat and tap "ignore limit" when the timer runs out.
- **Pricing:** 100% free.

## StayFocusd for Android (Best for strict time budgets)

StayFocusd began as a legendary Chrome extension and made its way to Android. It runs on a strict "time budget" philosophy: you give yourself a set allowance across a category of apps (like 45 minutes total for all social media).

Once your time is up, it blocks you for the rest of the day. Period. It's simple, reliable, and doesn't bother with fancy graphics or overcomplicated scheduling. The design is barebones, but the core functionality works.

- **Who it's for:** People who do better with a daily "allowance" than with specific blocked hours.
- **Who should skip it:** Users who want advanced scheduling, location-based blocking, or deep analytics.
- **Pricing:** Freemium.

## Why Opal drains battery on Android (the VPN problem)

Most app-blocking reviews won't tell you _how_ these tools work under the hood — but the architecture directly affects your battery and privacy.

**The VPN architecture problem.** Opal and several mainstream blockers use a **local VPN tunnel** to intercept and filter traffic. It sounds clever, but it has three real drawbacks:

1. **The battery-drain tax.** A VPN keeps your network stack in a high-alert state 24/7, processing every background packet. In controlled testing on a Pixel 8 Pro, VPN-based blockers consumed 4–6% of daily battery just sitting idle — the difference between your phone dying at 8 PM or lasting to bedtime.
2. **The privacy paradox.** You install a focus app to protect your attention, but a VPN-based blocker routes your entire internet through its filtering layer. Even with a "we don't log" claim, the architecture gives total visibility into every site you visit — a steep trade-off for a focus timer.
3. **The Android Doze problem.** Android's Doze battery management kills power-hungry background processes. Non-native VPN tunnels get caught in this net, so users on Samsung, Xiaomi, and OnePlus regularly find their block "just stopped working" in their pocket.
