**Blocking the app is the easy part, and it is not where blocks fail.** Social media on a phone is
four surfaces: the **app**, its **website**, a **second browser**, and a **second device**. Both
iPhone and Android give you a free tool that covers the first one well and the rest badly.

This page is about the other three.

## Why most social media blocks fail

You set an app limit on Instagram. It works for two days. On the third you open instagram.com in
Safari, and the limit has achieved nothing except a detour.

That is the pattern, and every variant of it is the same mistake — covering one surface and assuming
the others followed:

| The leak | What happens |
|---|---|
| **The website** | The app is blocked; the mobile site is identical and is not |
| **A second browser** | A site blocked in Safari opens fine in Firefox |
| **A second device** | The phone is locked down; the laptop and tablet are not |
| **The override** | The limit has a button that lifts it, and at 11pm you press it |

A plan that does not name all four in advance will fail at whichever one it forgot.

## On iPhone: Screen Time

Screen Time is genuinely good at apps and weaker everywhere else.

**App limits.** Settings → Screen Time → App Limits. You can limit a single app or the whole
**Social** category, which is the closest thing either platform has to a single switch.

**Downtime.** A scheduled window where only the apps you allow will open. Better than a daily total
for most people, because it targets the hours you actually lose.

**The leak you have to close.** By default a limit shows an **Ignore Limit** option when it runs out,
which keeps you going for the rest of the day. The **Block at End of Limit** toggle that removes it
only appears once you have set a **Screen Time passcode**
([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-us/HT208982)). Without that passcode, every limit on the device
is a suggestion.

**Websites.** Settings → Screen Time → **Content & Privacy Restrictions** → Content Restrictions →
Web Content, then add the sites to **Never Allow**. This is the step most people skip.

Two more holes worth knowing, both reported widely by parental-control vendors rather than documented
by Apple: **deleting and reinstalling an app can reset its limit** (close it by turning off *Deleting
Apps* under Content & Privacy Restrictions), and **changing the device time zone** has been used to
slip past time-based limits. Menu names move between iOS versions, so search Settings if yours differ.

## On Android: Digital Wellbeing

**App timers.** Settings → Digital Wellbeing & parental controls → Dashboard → an app → App timer.
The app greys out when the time runs out and comes back at midnight.

**Focus mode** pauses a chosen set of apps on demand, and **Bedtime mode** greys the screen on a
schedule.

**The leaks.** Three of them, and they are worse than iPhone's:

- **One app at a time.** There is no Social category. Ten apps means ten timers.
- **No URL awareness at all.** Digital Wellbeing counts app time and has no concept of a website, so
  the browser gap is total. Closing it needs
  [a blocker that works in the browser](/blog/how-to-block-websites-on-android-chrome).
- **The timer is a few taps from being raised**, in Settings, with no passcode option.

Android does have **Family Link** for a child's phone, where the parent controls the limits and the
child cannot change them — but its own site filter
[applies to Chrome and not to other browsers](/blog/how-to-block-websites-on-android-chrome).

A fuller comparison of the built-in tool against third-party ones is in
[Digital Wellbeing vs third-party focus apps](/blog/android-digital-wellbeing-vs-third-party-focus-apps).

## What each tool actually covers

| | iPhone Screen Time | Android Digital Wellbeing | A third-party blocker |
|---|---|---|---|
| Limit apps | Yes, individually or by category | Yes, one at a time | Yes |
| Block websites | Yes, via Content Restrictions | **No** | Yes, if it covers browsers |
| Covers every browser | Partly | n/a | Yes, if accessibility-based |
| Schedule by hours | Downtime | Bedtime, Focus mode | Weekly schedules |
| Override button | Ignore Limit, unless a passcode is set | None, but the timer is editable | Depends |
| Hard to switch off | With a Screen Time passcode | No | With a strict mode |
| Free | Yes | Yes | Usually freemium |

## Closing the gaps: a setup that holds

1. **Pick two or three apps, not all of them.** A total ban is a block you will resent by Thursday.
2. **Limit the apps** with Screen Time or Digital Wellbeing.
3. **Block the matching websites.** On iPhone, Never Allow. On Android, you need a third-party
   blocker, because the built-in tool cannot do it at all.
4. **Handle the second browser.** A block that only covers Safari or Chrome is one download from
   irrelevant. Either remove the others or use a tool that acts on all of them.
5. **Decide the override in advance.** Set a Screen Time passcode you do not memorise, or use a
   [strict mode](/blog/strict-mode-app-blocker-android) that makes the exit slow and deliberate
   instead of reflexive.

## Blocking by hours beats blocking by day

A daily cap can be spent in one sitting, usually the worst one. A **schedule** — blocked 9:00 to
17:00, and after 23:00 — leaves a clear window where the apps are fine, which is why people keep
them running instead of switching them off in frustration.

The setup for the work-hours version is in
[blocking social media during work hours](/blog/how-to-block-social-media-on-android-during-work-hours).

## What about just deleting the apps?

Deleting is the most popular answer and the least durable one. Reinstalling takes thirty seconds and
happens at exactly the moment the block existed for. Your account, your DMs and your feed are all
waiting. And the website was never touched.

Deleting works if the app is genuinely finished for you. If it is not, a block you cannot lift on
impulse does more than a deletion you can undo on one.

## The bottom line

Neither platform gives you one switch. iPhone covers apps and websites but hands you an override
unless you set a passcode. Android covers apps only, and the browser gap is total.

So block the apps, block their websites, make sure a second browser cannot serve them, and decide
before you need it what happens when you try to turn it all off. The first step is the one everybody
does. The other three are the ones that make it work.
