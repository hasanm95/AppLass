**The documented average cart abandonment rate is 70.22%.** That figure comes from the
[Baymard Institute](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate), which averaged 50 separate
studies published between 2006 and 2025. It has barely moved across two decades — which is the first
clue about how to read it.

Two things that number hides, and both change what you should do about it. Your Shopify admin is not
measuring the same thing. And a large share of those abandonments were never yours to win.

---

## What is the average cart abandonment rate in 2026?

**70.22%, averaged across 50 studies from 2006 to 2025.** Roughly seven in ten shoppers who add
something to a cart do not complete the purchase. The stability of the number over twenty years
matters more than its size: this is a structural property of online retail, not a defect your store
introduced.

That stability also means you should be suspicious of any source quoting a dramatically different
figure for 2026. Aggregations land between about 70% and 75% depending on which studies they
include and how they define a cart.

## Your Shopify abandoned checkout rate is a different metric

This is the single most common mistake merchants make with this benchmark, and it goes the
flattering direction — which is why nobody catches it.

**Shopify only records an abandoned checkout after the shopper enters identifying information,
usually an email address, and then leaves it incomplete for at least ten minutes.** The 70.22%
benchmark is measured from the moment something enters a cart.

| | Cart abandonment (the 70% benchmark) | Shopify abandoned checkouts |
|---|---|---|
| Starts counting at | Add to cart | Email entered at checkout |
| Requires contact details | No | Yes |
| Delay before it counts | None | 10 minutes |
| Who you can email | Nobody | Everybody in the report |
| Typical size | Large | Much smaller |

So if your Shopify admin suggests you are dramatically outperforming the benchmark, you are almost
certainly comparing a later, narrower funnel stage against a wider one. Both numbers are useful.
They are not comparable.

The practical consequence: the shoppers in Shopify's report are recoverable by email, because you
have their address. Everyone who bailed before that point can only be reached on-site — with cart
urgency, a [sticky add-to-cart bar](/blog/mobile-sticky-cart-guide), or an exit-intent offer.

## Cart abandonment by device

**Roughly 80% on mobile against 66–69% on desktop.** This split comes from Dynamic Yield's network
data, echoed by Contentsquare and SaleCycle — **not** from Baymard, which publishes no device
breakdown at all. A lot of articles attribute device figures to Baymard; they are wrong.

| Device | Reported range | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Mobile | 79.9–80.5% | Dynamic Yield, Contentsquare, SaleCycle |
| Desktop | 66.4–69.2% | Dynamic Yield, Contentsquare, SaleCycle |

Sources disagree by a couple of points, so the durable finding is the **gap of roughly 12
percentage points**, not any particular decimal. Mobile shoppers abandon far more, and since mobile
is the majority of traffic for most stores, your blended rate is largely a reflection of your
traffic mix rather than your checkout quality.

That also means a store whose rate "worsens" year over year may simply be getting more mobile
traffic. Segment by device before concluding anything went wrong.

## The part of the 70% you cannot fix

Here is the context almost every statistics roundup drops. Baymard **excludes** a browsing segment —
worth about **42% of all abandonments** — from its list of abandonment reasons, because those
shoppers were comparison shopping, saving items for later, or checking what delivery would cost,
with no intention of buying in that session.

Adding to cart is a bookmarking gesture as often as it is a purchase gesture. No checkout
optimisation reaches those people.

This reframes the goal. You are not trying to convert 70% of abandoners. You are working on the
remainder, which is why credible improvements are measured in single-digit percentage points, and
why any vendor promising to "cut abandonment by half" is describing something that has never
happened.

## Why the rest abandon, with numbers

Among shoppers who did have intent, Baymard's reasons are ranked and specific. Respondents could
choose more than one, so these do not total 100%.

| Reason for abandoning | Share |
|---|---|
| Extra costs too high (shipping, tax, fees) | 40% |
| Delivery was too slow | 20% |
| Didn't trust the site with card details | 19% |
| Site required account creation | 18% |
| Checkout too long or complicated | 17% |
| Website errors or crashes | 17% |
| Unsatisfactory returns policy | 13% |
| Couldn't see or calculate total cost upfront | 12% |
| Credit card declined | 10% |
| Not enough payment methods | 9% |

Read that list as a to-do list, because almost every line is a decision someone made rather than a
customer behaviour. Extra costs, forced accounts, a long checkout, hidden totals — those are
configuration, not fate. The top two alone are about **cost transparency and delivery expectations**,
which are set long before checkout.

## What actually moves the number at the cart stage

Ordered by how much of the list above they touch:

1. **Show the full cost early.** The single biggest reason, at 40%, is extra costs appearing late.
   A shipping estimate or a [free-shipping progress bar](/blog/free-shipping-bar-shopify) on the
   cart page pre-empts the shock.
2. **Remove the account wall.** Guest checkout addresses an 18% objection with one setting.
3. **Shorten the checkout.** Every removed field helps the 17% who leave because it is too long.
4. **Add deadline pressure honestly.** A [cart timer](/blog/shopify-cart-reservation-timer) converts
   "I'll come back later" into a decision now — as long as it describes a cart that expires rather
   than stock you are not really holding.
5. **Fix mobile first.** With a 12-point device gap, the same fix is worth more on a small screen.

## How to calculate a number you can actually compare

```
Cart abandonment rate = (1 − completed purchases ÷ carts created) × 100
```

Use carts created, not sessions and not checkouts started, or you will not be comparing against
70.22%. Then segment by device, because a blended figure hides the only split that reliably matters.

Track the trend, not the absolute. Your traffic mix, your channel mix and your product price all
push the absolute number around for reasons that have nothing to do with how good your checkout is.

## Bottom line

70.22% is the right benchmark, from 50 studies over twenty years. Just do not read it as a scoreboard.
About 42% of those abandonments were browsing, your Shopify report is measuring a different and later
stage, and the mobile-desktop gap explains more variance than most store-level differences do.

The useful version of this number is narrow: segment by device, compare against your own past,
and work down Baymard's reasons list from the top, because the first line item is worth double the
second.
