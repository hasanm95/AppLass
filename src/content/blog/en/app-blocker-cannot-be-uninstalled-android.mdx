**No app you install from the Play Store can truly stop you from uninstalling it.** Android removed
that capability in 2016 and has been closing the remaining gaps ever since. Every "unremovable" app
blocker is selling friction — extra steps, timed locks, and settings screens that close themselves
before you can tap the button.

That is not a reason to dismiss them. Friction is the entire point, and a blocker you can remove in
forty seconds of deliberate effort works very differently from one you can remove in two impulsive
taps. But you should know what you are buying, and if you are on the other side of this — stuck with
a blocker you want gone — you should know the exits.

---

## Can an app blocker really be impossible to uninstall?

**No, not on modern Android.** The ability for a device administrator to prevent its own removal was
taken out in Android 7 Nougat, for security reasons: malware was abusing it. Google then
[deprecated device admin entirely in 2020](https://developers.google.com/android/work/device-admin-deprecation),
and it is no longer available on devices with Google Mobile Services.

So when an app promises it "cannot be uninstalled," what it can actually deliver is delay. The
honest framing is a scale of resistance, not a binary.

| Method | Real resistance | How it is defeated |
|---|---|---|
| Plain app, no protection | None | Long-press, uninstall, ~5 seconds |
| PIN or password on settings | Low | You know the PIN |
| Accessibility service closes uninstall screen | Moderate | Safe mode, or revoke the permission |
| Device admin activated | Low to moderate, and shrinking | Deactivate admin, then uninstall |
| Timed lock with no early exit | Moderate — the strongest consumer option | Safe mode, ADB |
| Device Owner mode (enterprise) | Genuine | Factory reset only |

## How the accessibility trick actually works

Most blockers with uninstall protection use an **accessibility service**, the same permission that
lets them see which app is in the foreground. It watches the screen you are on. The moment you land
on the blocker's App Info page — the one with the uninstall button — or on the accessibility
settings where you could switch it off, the app closes or redirects that screen.

The important detail: this is interference with the uninstall flow, not a lock on it. Nothing at the
OS level is refusing your request. An app is racing you to the button and winning, because it reacts
in milliseconds and you do not.

That distinction explains every documented bypass. Boot into safe mode and Android does not run
third-party apps at all, so there is no service left to race you. The uninstall proceeds normally.

## The one method that genuinely works, and its cost

**Device Owner mode** is the real thing. It is the privileged mode enterprise device management
uses, and it can suspend applications, hide them from the launcher, block safe mode, and prevent
uninstall without authentication.

The catch is how you get there. Device Owner has to be provisioned on a device with no accounts
configured — in practice, straight after a factory reset — and set up over ADB from a computer. It
governs the entire device, not one app. A Play Store app cannot switch it on for you, which is
precisely why no consumer blocker offers it as a toggle.

If you have genuinely tried everything else and want a lock you cannot talk yourself out of, that is
the tier that exists. For almost everyone it is more than the problem requires.

## What to actually look for in a blocker

Given that no app can win the uninstall fight outright, the useful question changes. It is not "can
this be removed" — it can — but "how many deliberate steps stand between an impulse and a removal,
and does the app negotiate with you?"

The features that matter, in order:

1. **No early exit from an active session.** A timed lock with no override is the strongest thing a
   consumer app can offer, because it removes the negotiation rather than the capability. Mindful
   Guard's Strict Mode works this way: settings are locked for the duration you chose.
2. **No "just 5 more minutes" button.** Digital Wellbeing's timers fail here — one tap extends them,
   which is exactly the moment willpower is lowest.
3. **Friction on the settings path**, so switching off protection is not one tap either.
4. **Survives a reboot.** A blocker that quietly stops after a restart is decorative.

Note what is missing from that list: a promise of true unremovability. Any app making that claim
outright is describing something Android does not permit. Treat it the way you would treat any other
overstated spec.

## How to remove an app blocker you're stuck with

Half the people searching this phrase want the opposite of a blocker — they installed one, locked
themselves out, and now need it gone. In order, least disruptive first:

1. **Revoke the accessibility permission.** Settings → Accessibility → the app → off. If the screen
   closes itself, keep going.
2. **Deactivate device admin.** Settings → Security → Device admin apps → the app → deactivate.
3. **Boot into safe mode.** Hold the power button, then long-press "Power off" until the safe mode
   prompt appears. Third-party apps do not run. Uninstall from Settings → Apps, then reboot.
4. **Uninstall over ADB.** From a computer with USB debugging enabled:
   `adb uninstall <package.name>`. This bypasses the UI entirely.
5. **Factory reset.** Only necessary if the device is genuinely under Device Owner management, which
   for a self-installed focus app it will not be.

Steps 3 and 4 clear every consumer-grade blocker on the Play Store. If you are reaching for them
regularly, the blocker is not the problem worth solving — that is the subject of
[monk mode and the limits of willpower](/blog/monk-mode-willpower).

## Bottom line

"Cannot be uninstalled" is marketing language for "takes deliberate effort to uninstall," and on
Android that is the only thing on offer outside enterprise provisioning. The good news is that
deliberate effort is usually enough — the failure mode for focus apps is the impulsive tap, not the
determined one.

Pick the blocker with the strongest timed lock and the fewest negotiation points, and accept that
safe mode exists. If you find yourself in safe mode at 11pm to get one app back, the block worked
exactly as intended; it just told you something about the habit rather than the software.
