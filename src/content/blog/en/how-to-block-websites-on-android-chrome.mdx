**Chrome for Android has no setting that blocks a website.** There are five ways around that: a
filtering **Private DNS**, **Google Family Link** on a child's phone, a **VPN-based blocker**, the
**hosts file** if your phone is rooted, or an **app blocker that works through the accessibility
service**. Only the last one needs neither root nor a VPN.

Which you want depends on what you are willing to give up — your traffic, your root status, your
ability to change the setting back, or nothing much at all.

## Why Chrome on Android can't block sites by itself

On a desktop you install an extension and the problem is solved. Chrome for Android does not support
extensions, and it ships no block-a-site setting of its own. Its only site-level protection is Safe
Browsing, which warns about dangerous pages — not about pages you simply don't want to open at 11pm.

Android has no system-level URL blocklist either. So every method below works around the gap from a
different direction: the network, the account, the device, or the screen.

## The five methods, compared

| Method | Root? | VPN? | Covers | Per-site control | How hard to undo |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Accessibility blocker** | No | No | Every browser | Yes, per domain | Hard during a strict session |
| **Private DNS** | No | No | Whole device | Yes, via the resolver's list | ~5 seconds in Settings |
| **VPN blocker** | No | **Yes** | Whole device | Yes | Easy, disconnect the VPN |
| **Family Link** | No | No | **Chrome only** | Yes | Child can't; parent can |
| **Hosts file** | **Yes** | No | Whole device | Yes | Needs root access again |

## Method 1: Private DNS (free, no root, no app)

Android has a built-in setting that sends every domain lookup on the device to a resolver you choose.
Point it at a filtering resolver and the sites on its blocklist stop resolving everywhere at once —
Chrome, Firefox, Instagram's in-app browser, all of it.

1. Open **Settings → Network & internet → Private DNS**.
2. Choose **Private DNS provider hostname**.
3. Enter the hostname your filtering service gives you, for example a NextDNS configuration in the
   form `yourid.dns.nextdns.io`.
4. Add the sites you want blocked to that service's denylist.

**What it costs you:** it is a normal Android setting, so you can turn it off in about five seconds.
That makes it excellent against habit and useless against intent. It is also all-or-nothing in time —
there is no "block this between 9 and 5" — and it cannot distinguish the Instagram app from
instagram.com, because at the DNS layer they look identical.

**Best for:** filtering a whole household's phones, or blocking categories you never want on the
device at all.

## Method 2: Google Family Link (a child's phone)

If the phone belongs to a child with a supervised Google account, Family Link can filter sites from
the parent's phone and the child cannot change it back.

The limit people miss: **the Family Link site filter applies to Chrome, not to third-party browsers.**
Install Firefox or Samsung Internet and the filter is simply absent. The usual answer is to also block
the other browsers as apps, which works but turns into whack-a-mole.

**Best for:** parents, as one layer of several — not for limiting your own phone.

## Method 3: A VPN-based blocker

Several blockers route your traffic through a local VPN tunnel and drop requests to blocked domains.
It works, it needs no root, and it covers every app.

**What it costs you:** Android allows one VPN at a time, so this conflicts with a VPN you actually use
for privacy or work. More importantly, you are handing one app a view of all your network traffic —
which is a strange trade to make for a focus tool. Aggressive battery management on some Samsung,
Xiaomi and OnePlus devices also kills background VPNs, and the block quietly stops.

**Best for:** people who already trust a specific vendor and don't otherwise use a VPN.

## Method 4: The hosts file (root only)

The classic method: map blocked domains to `127.0.0.1` in `/etc/hosts` and they never resolve. On
modern Android that file is not writable without **root**, and rooting a phone to block a website
breaks your banking apps, voids warranties and weakens the device's security model.

Apps that advertise "hosts file blocking without root" are generally running a local VPN and editing
*its* hosts file, which puts you back in Method 3 with extra steps.

**Best for:** almost nobody in 2026.

## Method 5: An accessibility-based blocker (no root, no VPN)

The newest approach reads the address bar rather than the network. When a blocked domain loads in any
browser, the blocker notices and acts on it — no tunnel, no resolver, nothing leaving the device.

[Mindful Guard](/apps/mindful-guard) works this way:

1. Install Mindful Guard from the Play Store.
2. Allow the **Accessibility Service** when asked. This is what lets it see that a blocked domain has
   loaded in a tab.
3. Open a **focus session** or a **weekly schedule** and switch to the **Website** tab. Every blocked
   app's own site is already there and locked on — blocking the Instagram app also blocks
   instagram.com, because a block that covers one and not the other is not a block.
4. Add your own domains. Type one once and it is saved to your library; next time you just toggle it.
5. Start the block and test it. The blocked tab is redirected to a blank page and the **Pause Screen**
   appears, so you stay in the browser rather than being thrown out to the home screen.

**What it costs you:** the accessibility permission is broad, and you should treat that seriously —

## Blocking the app without the website is half a block

This is the failure mode worth naming, because it is the one almost everyone hits.

You put a timer on the Instagram app. The timer runs out. Thirty seconds later you are reading
instagram.com in Chrome, and the block has achieved nothing except an extra step. The same is true of
facebook.com, x.com, reddit.com, youtube.com and every other app whose website is a complete
substitute for it.

Android's own Digital Wellbeing cannot close this gap — it counts app time and knows nothing about
URLs. Any blocking plan that only covers apps has a browser-shaped hole in it. That is why the app
sites are pre-selected and locked rather than offered as an option.

## Which method should you use?

| Your situation | Method |
|---|---|
| You want a site gone from your own phone, on a schedule | Accessibility blocker |
| You want a category gone from every device in the house | Private DNS on each phone, or at the router |
| It's a child's phone | Family Link, plus Private DNS for the non-Chrome gap |
| You already run a VPN you trust | Private DNS, not a second VPN |
| You rooted the phone years ago and like it that way | Hosts file |
| You keep switching the block off | A blocker with [Strict Mode](/blog/app-blocker-cannot-be-uninstalled-android) |

## Can a website blocker be made impossible to remove?

No — and be wary of anything that claims otherwise. Android removed an app's ability to prevent its
own uninstall in 2016, so every "unremovable blocker" is really a friction layer: it makes the bypass
slow and deliberate rather than reflexive.

That is still worth having. A 20-minute wait is long enough for the urge that started it to pass,
which is the entire mechanism. What it is not is a lock, and a tool that pretends to be one is setting
you up to trust it at the wrong moment. The honest version of this is in
[app blockers that can't be uninstalled](/blog/app-blocker-cannot-be-uninstalled-android).

## Why does a website blocker need the accessibility service?

Because seeing which page another app has loaded is exactly what Android's accessibility APIs are
for — the same APIs a screen reader uses. There is no narrower permission that would do the job.

It is a broad permission, so apply the obvious test before granting it to anything: does the app need
an account? Does it have network access? What does its Google Play **Data safety** section declare?
A focus tool that reads your address bar should be sending nothing anywhere, and you should be able
to verify that rather than take it on faith — the argument for
[a blocker with no telemetry at all](/blog/zero-telemetry-blocker).

## The bottom line

Chrome for Android cannot block a website, so pick the trade you prefer. **Private DNS** is free and
system-wide but five seconds from being switched off. **Family Link** is right for a child's phone and
stops at Chrome. A **VPN blocker** costs you your VPN slot and shows one vendor all your traffic. The
**hosts file** costs you root. An **accessibility-based blocker** costs you a broad permission and
gives back per-domain, per-schedule blocking in every browser.

Whichever you choose, block the app and its website together — or you have built a speed bump and
called it a wall.
