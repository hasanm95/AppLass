**The mechanism is safe. Individual apps vary, and you can tell them apart in about a minute.**

Every app blocker on Android asks for the **accessibility service**, because it is the only thing
Android offers that can notice an app opening. That permission is genuinely broad — it can read
what is on your screen. So "is this blocker safe" is a real question, not paranoia.

It is also an answerable one.

## What the accessibility service actually grants

Android is unusually blunt about this in its own warning dialogue, and the warning is accurate. An
app with the accessibility service can:

- **Read the content of windows** — the text on screen in other apps, including what is in the
  address bar
- **Observe your actions** — taps, typing, navigation
- **Perform actions on your behalf** — press buttons, navigate, go back

This is what makes screen readers possible for blind and low-vision users. It is also, obviously,
what makes it worth a second's thought before granting.

Malware has abused it, which is why Android shows that warning at all and why newer Android versions
have made the grant flow deliberately awkward.

## Why every blocker needs it anyway

To stop you opening Instagram, an app must know you opened Instagram. To redirect a blocked site, it
must see that the site loaded in a tab.

Android provides **no narrower permission for either**. There is no "tell me which app is in the
foreground, and nothing else" API available to ordinary apps. So the options are:

| Approach | Permission needed | What it costs you |
|---|---|---|
| **Accessibility service** | Broad: can read the screen | Trust in one app, locally |
| **VPN-based** | Routes all network traffic | Trust in one app, with all your traffic |
| **Digital Wellbeing** | None, it is the OS | Cannot block websites or feeds |

Notice the middle row. People sometimes avoid accessibility by choosing a VPN-based blocker, which
is a **larger** ask, not a smaller one — it moves the trust from "what is on my screen" to "every
request my phone makes".

## Four checks that actually tell you something

Do these on the Play listing before you install, not after.

**1. Read the Data safety section.** Scroll down on the Play listing. You want **"No data
collected."** A focus app that declares it collects device identifiers, usage data or your email is
telling you there is a server, and a reason it wants to reach it.

**2. Expand the permission list.** A blocker needs accessibility and very little else. **Contacts,
location, SMS and call logs have nothing to do with blocking apps.** Their presence is not proof of
anything bad, but it is a question the developer should have answered somewhere.

**3. Does it force you to create an account?** An account means a server, and a server is a place
data can sit and be breached. "No account required" is a structural reduction in risk, not a
marketing line.

**4. Turn off its network access and use it for a day.** Settings → Apps → the app → Mobile data &
Wi-Fi → off. A genuinely local blocker keeps working perfectly. One that breaks, or starts nagging,
is doing something over the network that you should understand before you hand it your screen.

That fourth check is the strongest one, because it does not depend on believing anybody's
declaration.

## What "safe" cannot mean

Being straight about the limits of all this:

- **A Data safety declaration is self-reported.** Google does not audit it. It is a statement of
  intent that carries Play Store consequences if false, which is better than nothing and less than
  proof.
- **Nobody can prove a negative.** No app can demonstrate it has never sent anything. The best it
  can do is have no server to send to, and let you verify that by cutting its network.
- **Open source helps, but only if someone reads it.** Published code that nobody audits is a
  stronger signal of intent than of safety.

So the realistic goal is not certainty. It is reducing how much you have to trust: an app that
collects nothing, needs no account and works with its network off has very little room to misbehave,
whatever its intentions.

## Red flags worth walking away from

- Permissions with no relationship to blocking: contacts, SMS, call logs, precise location
- A mandatory account for a feature that is obviously local
- A Data safety section that declares collection the app never explains
- Pressure to grant **Device admin**, which is a separate and much heavier permission than
  accessibility
- Breaking when you turn its network off

## The bottom line

Every Android blocker needs the same broad permission, so "it uses accessibility" tells you nothing
about a specific app. What tells you something is the Data safety declaration, the other permissions,
whether it demands an account, and whether it survives losing network access.

Four checks, about a minute, and most of the question answered.
